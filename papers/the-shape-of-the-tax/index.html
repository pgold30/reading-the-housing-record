Paper 03 · Tax design

The Shape of the Tax

Round Prices and Bunching at New York City's 2019 Transfer-Tax Notches

Pablo LoschiEmpirical research

The short version

After the 2019 reform, the share of sales at exactly $2, $3 or $5 million fell by about 60%, with more transactions recorded just below the new tax lines. Comparing those prices before and after the reform separates this movement from existing round-price spikes. The records cannot reveal each buyer’s negotiation or who ultimately bears the tax.

The question

Many housing transaction taxes are notches: once the price crosses a threshold, the higher rate applies to the whole price. New York's 2019 budget created seven buyer-paid notches on one date, from $2 million to $25 million, and left the $1 million mansion tax unchanged. What does a schedule of notches do to sales, and what does that mean for the shape of a housing tax?

The paper compares prices at the same thresholds before and after the reform, against the unchanged $1 million threshold and never-taxed round prices. It also examines the loan notch in New York City's mortgage recording tax, whose standard borrower schedule is 1.80–1.925% of recorded mortgage principal, before credits, exemptions and consolidation adjustments.

Main findings

  • 7.8 → 2.3sales at exactly $2 million relative to local density, before and after it became a taxed threshold
  • ≈60%fall in the share of sales at exactly $2, $3 or $5 million; those sales closed on average $2,000–3,000 below the threshold, less than the tax saved, for tax jumps from $5,000 to $37,500 alike
  • −22/−28%sales in the 5% above the $3 million and $5 million thresholds, beyond the round price, net of the unchanged $1 million threshold and never-taxed round prices; no detectable change at $2 million
  • 0.22→0.04the standard bunching estimate at $2 million: 0.223 with all sales, 0.041 without the sales at exactly $2 million and one dollar below, within the range of never-taxed prices
  • $1.6–2.9Ma year of avoided tax at the new notches, depending on how the moved sales are counted: under 1% of the roughly $296 million a year the reform's schedule implies
  • 1.41%of price for houses and 1.28% for condominiums: the gross recording charge the schedule implies on the financed sample, before credits and exemptions
  • $244Ma year: a fixed-sample benchmark: the gross charge the schedule implies on four-borough residential purchase money, transactions held fixed, before credits, exemptions and consolidation adjustments; 98% of it measured on actual loans; about half of the residential liability the city reports

The paper compares the new thresholds with 24 never-taxed round prices and checks the timing year by year. Existing research already documents round-price spikes; the 2019 reform lets this study follow the same prices as their tax treatment changes. The share that moved is lower when a company sells and close to one at the $1 million threshold, taxed since 1989. Standard bunching widths, which assume a smooth price distribution, read the round-price moves as much larger price cuts. The tax figures are gross schedule-implied charges and fixed-transaction costings. They do not identify who ultimately bears the tax, how sales would respond to a continuous tax, or an optimal rate.

Why it matters

  • Tax thresholds are almost always round numbers. At a round threshold, much of the measured bunching is deals that would have closed at the round price closing just below it, so estimates that ignore the round price overstate how far prices move.
  • Even a quarter-point notch moves the deals that would have closed at the round price, so the shape of a schedule matters as well as its level. Slice schedules could raise the same revenue without jumps.
  • Debates over taxing cash purchases rarely start from the fact that the code already charges the financed buyer. That statutory difference can be measured directly from loans, without relying on a contested price gap.
  • A fixed-transaction costing gives legislators the order of magnitude of a purchase-money carve-out and of continuous schedules that match it within the sample.

What the results can tell us

The threshold comparisons describe transaction shifting around the reform. The mortgage-tax figures apply the statutory schedule to observed loans before credits, exemptions and consolidation adjustments. Replacement costings hold transactions fixed; they do not identify economic incidence, behavioural responses to a replacement or an optimal tax rate.
