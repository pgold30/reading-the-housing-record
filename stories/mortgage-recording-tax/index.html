Same $900,000 condo, $13,860 more in tax for the buyer with a mortgage

A $720,000 mortgage brings a gross recording charge of $13,860 at closing. A buyer paying cash for the same condo avoids that charge.

By Pablo Loschi9 October 2026Updated 15 November 20264 min readSources & methods

Suppose two buyers close on the same $900,000 condo. One pays cash. The other puts 20% down and borrows $720,000. At closing, recording that mortgage triggers $13,860 in tax on top of the down payment—before any credits or consolidation breaks. The cash buyer pays zero.

Only the loan triggers the bill. Unlike the $1 million mansion tax, New York’s mortgage recording tax hits at every price, right when a borrowing buyer is tightest on cash.

What this means for a buyer

If you borrow to buy a house or condo in NYC, budget separately for the recording tax on the mortgage itself (credits, exemptions or a CEMA loan assignment can reduce the final bill; co-op share loans are exempt).

A charge on the loan

The mortgage recording tax combines New York State and City levies. The City’s guidance explains the recording requirement and directs readers to ACRIS for a calculation. The tax concerns recording a mortgage against real property. Financing secured by shares in an individual co-op apartment falls outside it.

For the residential loans studied in the paper, the standard borrower schedule is 1.80% below $500,000 and 1.925% at or above that loan amount. Those percentages apply to the mortgage principal. The $900,000 condo example uses the higher rate on a $720,000 loan.

My example applies the schedule to the whole recorded loan. A real bill can be lower: credits or exemptions may apply, and a consolidation, extension and modification agreement, often called a CEMA, can shrink the taxable amount. The paper does not apply those adjustments.

What the recorded loans add up to

My study links sales and recorded mortgages in Manhattan, the Bronx, Brooklyn and Queens from 2016 to 2025. On 174,732 purchases with observed loan amounts, the standard borrower schedule works out to an average of 1.41% of the purchase price for houses and 1.28% for condos, before credits, exemptions and consolidation agreements. For financed purchases between $300,000 and $6 million in those four boroughs, filling in loan amounts where they are missing, the total comes to about $248 million a year.

A charge only borrowers pay

A buyer who pays cash skips the tax entirely. A buyer who borrows pays it, and the less they have saved, the bigger the loan and the bigger the bill. Co-op buyers who borrow pay nothing either, because their loans are secured by shares rather than real property. That raises a plain question: should needing a loan make buying a home more expensive?

In May 2026, Albany considered a 1% tax on all-cash purchases of homes above $1 million. The city comptroller reported in June that the proposal was left out of the enacted state budget. The recording tax already distinguishes between buying with cash and borrowing, including below $1 million.

On the same $300,000-to-$6 million house and condo market across those four boroughs, a flat 0.87% charge on every sale would match the $248 million static total. Across the tested schedules, roughly nine in ten financed buyers would pay around $5,000 less on average, while unfinanced sales would pick up about $11,000 to $13,000. Those static comparisons hold sales volume fixed, apply before credits, and cannot measure who ultimately bears the economic burden—or tell whether an unmatched buyer truly paid cash rather than using private borrowing.
