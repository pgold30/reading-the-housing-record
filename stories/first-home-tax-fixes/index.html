Four ways New York could make a first home cheaper to buy

The city's housing shortage will take years to fix. Its tax on mortgages, the cliffs in its transfer tax and gaps in its sales data could change much sooner.

By Pablo Loschi18 October 2026Updated 26 October 20267 min readSources & methods

Two families make offers on the same $900,000 condominium. One pays cash. The other has saved a 20% down payment and borrows the remaining $720,000. Under the standard schedule used in my research, recording that loan brings a gross borrower charge of $13,860 upfront, before credits, exemptions or consolidation adjustments. The cash buyer avoids that charge.

What this means for a buyer

In this example, the $13,860 charge is another 7.7% of the family’s $180,000 down payment. Other taxes jump when a purchase price reaches a threshold. The proposals below would change those charges; the research does not establish how they would affect home prices or first-time ownership.

New York’s deeper problem is a housing shortage that will take years to build out of. The city’s rental vacancy rate was 1.41% in 2023, according to the Housing and Vacancy Survey. On 1 October, the mayor’s administration announced a faster approval route for affordable housing in 12 community districts, due to take effect in January 2027. Those districts produced just 724 of the city’s 63,870 new affordable homes over the preceding five years.

The city can also look at what a buyer pays at closing. My research links New York City sales from 2016 through 2025 to deeds and mortgages and looks at how the tax rules shape recorded transactions. It points to four changes worth considering. None of them replaces building more homes, and the records do not show which buyers are buying for the first time.

1. Move the tax off the mortgage

I think it is unfair that a family borrowing to live in a home pays the recording charge while a company buying the same home for cash does not. That is my view, not a finding: the records do not show that every company purchase is speculative, or that every cash buyer lives elsewhere.

For the residential loans in my study, the borrower's rate is 1.80% on loans below $500,000 and 1.925% at or above it, charged on the loan rather than the price. The bigger the loan, the bigger the charge. Loans on co-op shares are not covered, and credits or consolidation agreements can lower a real bill.

The tax could be raised another way. On financed house and condo purchases between $300,000 and $6 million in four boroughs, the recording charge comes to about $248 million a year in my corrected estimate, filling in loan amounts where they are missing. A charge of about 0.872% on every purchase, borrowed or not, would raise the same amount from the same sales. On the $900,000 condo, that is about $7,850 instead of $13,860. Exempting the first $500,000 of each price and charging about 1.55% on the rest would make it about $6,200. The borrowing family would save roughly $6,000 or $7,700, before other adjustments.

Under the schedules I tested, about nine in ten financed purchases would pay less. These figures hold sales and prices fixed, so they do not show that a replacement would bring in the same money in practice. Part of today's tax also goes to the MTA, and any change would have to deal with that. More on the recording tax.

2. Charge each rate on a slice of the price

New York’s “slab” taxes apply a higher rate to the entire amount when a loan or price reaches a threshold. Under the gross schedule, borrowing $500,000 instead of $499,999 adds about $625. At a $1 million residential purchase price, the 1% mansion tax switches on and adds $10,000 relative to a price one dollar below it. Reaching $2 million adds about $5,000 in buyer tax; reaching $5 million adds about $37,500. Exemptions and qualifying older contracts sit outside this illustration.

After higher buyer-tax tiers began at $2 million, $3 million and $5 million in 2019, the share of sales at those exact prices fell by about 60%, while sales just below them increased. That pattern is consistent with a response to the new tax steps. It does not show where any individual sale would otherwise have closed. A separate New Jersey comparison finds that the share at exactly $1 million fell 91% after its 2004 buyer fee, even though a sale at that exact price was exempt. The full story of the $2 million line.

A marginal schedule charges each rate only on the slice of the price above its threshold. England’s stamp duty changed to that structure in December 2014. For New York sales of $2 million or more since 2020, the paper constructs a schedule matching the roughly $296 million annual gross benchmark on those same transactions, without one-dollar cliffs. About 7% of those high-end sales would face a higher calculated charge. Actual revenue would depend on how transactions respond.

3. Check the cash discount before taxing it

A surcharge on all-cash purchases was considered in the May 2026 budget talks. The city comptroller’s June report says the proposal was left out of the enacted state budget. That leaves a question: what evidence should lawmakers require before choosing a tax based on the buyer’s financing?

One argument for a cash surcharge is that paying cash wins buyers a lower price than families who borrow get. In my repeat-sales comparison, houses bought without a mortgage match do sell for about 9% less. But the gap falls from 9.25 to 4.87 log points when deeds where buyer and seller share a surname are set aside. That shows how much the number depends on which sales are counted; it does not show that relatives cause half of it.

The forms filed with the deeds back up the screen. In a stratified sample of 60 transfer reports, 16 of 40 same-surname deeds declared a sale between relatives or showed another non-market sign, against 2 of 20 other cash-labelled deeds. The reports were read with AI assistance, and I checked every reading against the form. When the cash buyer is a company, the gap is smaller, at 1.53 log points, but its interval runs from −1.79 to 5.44, so a gap of several log points cannot be ruled out. How the cash discount changes.

A purchase tax that ignores how a buyer pays would remove the difference in the law directly. Whether that is better depends on who the city wants to help and on how prices and borrowing would respond, which my research does not measure.

4. Publish the deed number and flag non-market transfers

The RP-5217NYC transfer report asks whether a sale is between relatives, involves a buyer who is also a seller, or has other unusual conditions. The city’s public sales file does not include those answers or the ACRIS deed document number. Publishing them would let users check which transactions belong in a market-price comparison without opening scanned forms one by one.

The matching rules need scrutiny too. An incorrect unit-lot ceiling in my code caused the previously reported Bronx condominium gap. Correcting it raised linkage from 51.7% to 98.8%; the corrected dataset is now public. That is a correction to my method, not evidence of missing public deeds. What changed.

Some foreclosure transfers to lenders also reach the sales file at amounts set by a debt or a legal rule. My deed paper shows that screening them out changes the path of a repeat-sales price index, in a direction that depends on the local rule. It does not give a correction for any single home's value. Clear transfer-condition fields would make these cases easier to spot. Foreclosure deeds in the sales file.

Limits to keep in mind

These proposals concern the bill at closing and the records used to measure the market. They will not build an apartment. Their revenue comparisons hold transactions fixed, and the relationship screens are imperfect. I would still ask lawmakers to explain why borrowing for a home should trigger a charge that paying cash avoids, and to publish the information needed to judge the alternatives.
