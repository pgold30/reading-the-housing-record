The cash-buyer discount shrinks by half when likely family sales are left out

In New York City records, houses bought for cash sell for about 9% less. Leave out sales where buyer and seller share a surname and the gap halves.

By Pablo Loschi1 October 2026Updated 15 November 20264 min readSources & methods

Suppose the same New York City house sells twice between 2016 and 2025—once to a buyer with a mortgage, once to an all-cash buyer. On paper, the cash sale closes for about 9% less. When Albany weighed a 1% tax on $1 million-plus cash purchases in May 2026, that gap looked like pure closing-certainty discount. Look closer at the deeds, though, and half of it disappears.

What this means for a buyer

The headline "cash discount" in public records is not a clean benchmark for how much lower an all-cash offer can win by. It mixes financing with related-party transfers and missed mortgage links.

Across repeat house sales linked in my study, financed deals close 9.25 log points higher than unfinanced ones—roughly an 8.8% cash discount.

Set aside the 358 repeat-sale pairs where buyer and seller share a surname, however, and the gap cuts nearly in half to 4.87 log points (roughly 5%).

Who is in the cash group?

Shared surnames catch many family deals, though they miss relatives with different names and occasionally match strangers. Transfer forms confirm the pattern: in a stratified sample of 60 cash deeds (40 same-surname, 20 other cash) screened with AI and verified one by one against the forms, 16 of 40 same-surname deeds declared a transfer between relatives or another non-market sign, against only 2 of 20 other cash deeds. One was a $1.6 million transfer that stayed in the family.

Corporate cash buyers behave differently again: when the buyer is a company, the contrast shrinks to 1.53 log points (about 1.5%), with a wide 95% interval from −1.79 to +5.44 log points—leaving even the sign uncertain. Many of those "companies" are ordinary buyers purchasing through personal LLCs rather than large institutional investors.

Comparing the same house twice holds fixed attributes constant, though it still misses renovations or distressed terms between sales.

Finding a mortgage is a separate problem

Unmatched does not always mean all-cash. When 53 sale pairs were audited, 4 of 50 sampled "cash" deals turned out to carry a same-day purchase mortgage—two misdated by a year in the city's index and two secured against an adjoining parcel.

How much depends on labelling errors

Dropping those four mislabelled pairs nudges the headline gap from 9.25 to 9.40 log points, and pairing documented corrections with the surname screen yields 5.19 log points (95% interval 2.98 to 7.18). Simulating random mislabelling shrinks the estimate slightly without erasing it, though a small audit cannot rule out non-random errors clustered in one direction.

A targeted check also turned up three multi-parcel bundles and four index-date mismatches; applying general rules for those puts the estimate at 9.91 log points. One deed cross-reference even turned out to be a power of attorney.

Before citing a raw "cash discount" in negotiations—or pricing a policy around it—check what sits underneath the cash label. Once family deals and corporate purchases are separated, public deeds leave surprisingly little hard proof of a large closing-certainty premium.
